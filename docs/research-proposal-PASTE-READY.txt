Proposed
Research Title 2
(not more than 25 words)

Development and Evaluation of an AI-Integrated Web-Based Smart Diagnostic Breadboard Trainer as an Instructional Tool for Enhancing Grade 12 Learners' Electronics Circuit Troubleshooting Performance


Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) Addressed

The AI-Integrated Web-Based Smart Diagnostic Breadboard Trainer primarily supports SDG 4 - Quality Education. It responds directly to Target 4.4, which calls for substantially increasing the number of youth with relevant technical and vocational skills for employment and decent work, because the study develops and evaluates an instructional tool for teaching one specific employable competency, namely the systematic troubleshooting of electronic circuits, to Grade 12 learners in the Technical-Vocational-Livelihood track. It likewise responds to Target 4.a, which calls for education facilities that provide effective learning environments, because the trainer allows learners to build circuits, identify incorrect connections, locate faults on the board, and receive an explanation of the probable cause in language they can understand and act upon. This makes troubleshooting practicable inside a Grade 12 electronics class where one teacher handles a large section and cannot inspect every board. Because the trainer is web-based and runs in an ordinary browser without installation, learners may repeat the practice cycle without consuming components and may practice outside laboratory hours, and schools with limited laboratory equipment are not excluded from the activity. It further responds to Target 4.5 on equal access to vocational training for the vulnerable. In many Philippine public schools, learners are grouped for electronics activities so that the cost of components can be shouldered together, with the result that only one member of the group ordinarily performs the hands-on work. Because each learner works on an individual board in the trainer, the opportunity to practice no longer depends on a learner's capacity to contribute to the cost of materials. The trainer is intended to supplement rather than replace the physical laboratory, extending access to practice for both learners and teachers.

The study also supports SDG 9 - Industry, Innovation and Infrastructure, particularly Target 9.5 on enhancing scientific research and upgrading technological capabilities, because it applies both rule-based diagnostic technology and artificial intelligence to a long-standing instructional problem in electronics education. The trainer detects and locates a fault through direct comparison with a reference circuit and then uses an artificial intelligence model to explain that fault to the learner, an application of emerging technology to technical education that remains uncommon in Philippine public schools. The competencies the trainer develops, namely systematic fault isolation and evidence-based reasoning, are foundational to the skilled technical workforce on which industrial infrastructure depends.


Rationale
(not more than 300 words; include supporting literature)

In Philippine public schools, electronics instruction in the Technical-Vocational-Livelihood track is constrained by the cost of components. Learners are commonly grouped so the expense can be shouldered together, but within a group only one learner usually builds the circuit while the rest observe. Hands-on practice is unevenly distributed, and troubleshooting, the least reliably taught skill, suffers most. Kinsel et al. (2024), analyzing fifty-three think-aloud debugging sessions, found only 42% of students located all planted faults and 38% resorted to random tinkering rather than systematic tracing. Loubser et al. (2024) found teachers' feedback during practical tasks dominated by low-level questioning offering little diagnostic guidance.

Technology addresses parts of this. Bianchi et al. (2024) developed BlinkBoard, an augmented breadboard guiding assembly, and Calixto et al. (2026) developed TRACE, a breadboard-based tester detecting defective components. Faina (2022) built a simulator whose error detection lets learners self-correct unaided, while Li and Liang (2024), meta-analyzing forty-six studies, found virtual laboratories moderately beneficial (Hedges' g = 0.686). On feedback, Zhao et al. (2025) meta-analyzed twenty-nine experiments and found generative artificial intelligence improves higher-order thinking (g = 0.609), more strongly at K to 12 level, and Dai et al. (2025) showed in two randomized trials that AI heuristic hints raised lower-performing high school students' achievement where direct answers did not.

A gap remains. No reviewed tool compares a learner's completed circuit with a correct reference, locates the discrepancy on the board, and then uses artificial intelligence to explain its probable cause and guide the learner toward the correction. Reviewed studies also involve university, not senior high, learners.

The proposed trainer pairs rule-based fault detection with AI-generated, hint-first feedback. It does not replace the physical laboratory but makes practice accessible, giving every learner, not one member per group, a board to build and troubleshoot on.


Objectives of the Study

The study aims to develop and evaluate an AI-Integrated Web-Based Smart Diagnostic Breadboard Trainer as an instructional tool to improve Grade 12 learners' skills in circuit assembly, fault identification, and troubleshooting.

Specifically, the study aims to:

1. Develop an AI-Integrated Web-Based Smart Diagnostic Breadboard Trainer that can assist learners in:
1.1 assembling basic electronic circuits;
1.2 identifying incorrect or missing circuit connections;
1.3 locating connection errors at a specific point on the board;
1.4 receiving AI-generated diagnostic feedback that explains a detected circuit problem in language appropriate to the learner; and
1.5 working toward the correction through hints before the answer is revealed.

2. Determine the level of functionality of the developed Smart Diagnostic Breadboard Trainer, as assessed by expert evaluators, in terms of:
2.1 circuit connection detection;
2.2 fault identification;
2.3 fault localization;
2.4 accuracy and appropriateness of AI-generated diagnostic feedback; and
2.5 ease of operation.

3. Determine the level of acceptability of the Smart Diagnostic Breadboard Trainer as perceived by the selected respondents in terms of:
3.1 functionality;
3.2 usability;
3.3 accuracy;
3.4 reliability; and
3.5 instructional value.

4. Determine the perceived effectiveness of the Smart Diagnostic Breadboard Trainer in supporting learners' development of:
4.1 circuit assembly skills;
4.2 component identification;
4.3 fault identification; and
4.4 troubleshooting skills.

5. Determine whether significant differences exist in the perceived acceptability of the Smart Diagnostic Breadboard Trainer when the respondents are grouped according to type of respondent.

6. Identify the problems and difficulties encountered by the respondents in using the Smart Diagnostic Breadboard Trainer and use the findings as a basis for further improvement of the device.


Review of Literature
(minimum of 10 literature and studies that are at least 5 years back)

Breadboarding is an important part of circuit prototyping and serves as a method for familiarizing students with fundamental electronics concepts. It provides learners with a practical environment where electronic components can be assembled, tested, and modified before being transferred to a more permanent circuit board. Owen et al. (2026) emphasized the value of breadboarding in electronics learning and presented an instructional sequence in which secondary learners progress from breadboard prototyping to printed circuit board design, arguing that the breadboard stage is what makes the later and less forgiving stage understandable to students. Their work situates breadboarding not as a preliminary activity but as the phase in which conceptual understanding of a circuit is actually formed.

Although conventional breadboards are useful for prototyping, researchers have explored alternative designs to address their physical limitations. Sun et al. (2022) identified the inflexibility of conventional breadboards and introduced JellyBoard, a flexible breadboard that can change shape under applied force and can be constructed using accessible materials. This design provides greater adaptability for electronic prototyping, particularly in applications requiring flexible or soft interfaces. Similarly, Ko et al. (2023) introduced FlexBoard, a prototyping platform built on a living-hinge pattern with inserted terminal strips that allows interactive components such as sensors, actuators, and displays to be integrated into curved and flexible surfaces while preserving standard pin spacing. These studies demonstrate that breadboard technology can be modified to make circuit prototyping more flexible and suitable for different learning and application environments. What they do not address, however, is the difficulty a learner faces once the board has been built and the circuit does not work.

That difficulty has been studied directly. Kinsel et al. (2024) analyzed fifty-three think-aloud interviews in which sophomore engineering students attempted to locate four deliberately planted faults in breadboarded circuits containing resistors, capacitors, operational amplifiers, and diodes. They reported a completion rate of only 42%. The strategies most frequently observed were tracing, testing, pattern matching, and drawing on domain knowledge, while rebuilding, chunking, using instruments, analytical reasoning, and isolation were rarely used. Most notably, 38% of the participants relied on random tinkering, that is, manipulating the circuit without a working hypothesis. The researchers concluded that understanding how students actually troubleshoot should inform laboratory instruction. If university engineering students troubleshoot unsystematically, the assumption that senior high school learners will acquire the skill incidentally cannot be sustained. Loubser et al. (2024) observed a matching deficit on the teaching side. Studying five technology teachers as they gave formative feedback while learners worked through practical design tasks, they found that feedback consisted predominantly of low-level questions, with generative design questions and substantive comments least frequently observed. Their finding locates the problem in the quality and immediacy of the feedback available during practical work.

The integration of technology into breadboards has been explored to provide learners with additional support during circuit construction. Bianchi et al. (2024) developed BlinkBoard, an augmented breadboard designed to guide and monitor students during electronic circuit assembly. Built as an Arduino shield carrying a printed breadboard and fifty LEDs positioned beside each row, the system uses four blinking patterns to guide the placement of components and wires, while input and output pins sense voltage at specified rows. An open communication protocol links each learner's board to an instructor dashboard, supporting both synchronous and remote instruction. Their work demonstrated that technology-assisted guidance can reduce students' dependence on a teaching assistant for routine wiring problems and allow them to focus on understanding circuit behavior.

Beyond guiding students during assembly, recent research has also focused on helping learners identify problems with electronic components. Calixto et al. (2026) introduced TRACE, a breadboard-based electronic component tester developed to identify components, measure their electrical characteristics, and evaluate their condition in real time. The system can test components such as resistors, capacitors, LEDs, transistors, and basic logic gates, providing beginners with immediate information that would otherwise require a separate instrument or the assistance of an instructor, and preventing the error of building a circuit around a component that was already defective. Boyd and Tow (2026) approached the breadboard from the standpoint of accessibility, reporting that an adaptive breadboard design increased opportunities for learners in basic circuits and measurement classes by allowing students to construct circuits more autonomously and by reducing limitations associated with fine-motor abilities. Their work reframes breadboard design as a question of who is able to participate, which is directly relevant to an inclusive senior high school classroom.

Computer-based environments have likewise been shown to support electronics learning. Faina (2022) developed a simulator for Fritzing that reproduces the laboratory experience for learners studying from home, including realistic multimeter behavior and error-detection features that identify common student mistakes so that learners can correct themselves. The author reported that the tool retained its value as a complement to in-person instruction even after remote teaching ended. Evidence on the effectiveness of such environments is now substantial. Li and Liang (2024) meta-analyzed forty-six independent studies drawn from twenty-two publications on virtual laboratories in engineering education and found a moderate overall effect (Hedges' g = 0.686), with the largest effects on learning motivation and engagement, moderate effects on knowledge acquisition, cognitive ability, and operational skill, and a reduction in cognitive load. The authors cautioned that virtual laboratories cannot fully replace hands-on work and recommended deliberate integration of the two.

At the classroom level, Asbendri et al. (2024) conducted a quasi-experimental study with Grade 11 electronics engineering students using a non-equivalent control group design. The group taught with Tinkercad-assisted learning media improved by 11.5% over the group that received direct instruction, and the difference was statistically significant. The study is relevant because its participants are senior secondary vocational learners, the population closest to Grade 12 TVL learners. Moelans et al. (2024) surveyed seventy-nine university students working in a virtual experimental environment and found that self-regulated learning was significantly related to engagement, effort, and satisfaction. The researchers emphasized the importance of learning environments that support students' ability to regulate their own learning and recommended personalized and adaptive features. Their finding qualifies the optimism of the preceding studies, since a computer-based laboratory benefits learners who can already regulate their own learning, which argues for a tool that supplies structure and prompting rather than an open workspace.

Artificial intelligence has more recently been applied to the feedback itself. Zhao et al. (2025) meta-analyzed twenty-nine experimental and quasi-experimental studies comprising fifty-nine effect sizes and found that generative artificial intelligence produced a moderate positive effect on students' higher-order thinking (Hedges' g = 0.609), with the largest gain in problem-solving ability (g = 0.745). Two of their moderators bear on this study. Effects were stronger at the K to 12 level (g = 0.857) than in post-secondary education, and learners with high self-regulation gained far more (g = 0.863) than those with low self-regulation (g = 0.284), a pattern consistent with Moelans et al. (2024). Guo et al. (2025) integrated a large language model into a four-week science and engineering practice unit for middle school students in a quasi-experiment and reported significant improvements in scientific knowledge, attitudes toward science, computational thinking, and problem-solving relative to a control class taught conventionally. The form of the feedback matters, however. Dai et al. (2025) conducted two randomized controlled trials with 387 high school physics students and found that lower-performing students gained in achievement when an AI system provided heuristic solution hints but not when it provided conventional answers, while higher-performing students benefited most when they controlled when to request help. Kimmel et al. (2024) integrated GPT-4 explanations of compiler, runtime, and logic errors into an automated assessment tool for programming students and concluded that adding generative artificial intelligence to an automated tool does not by itself make the tool better, since the design of the interface largely determines whether learners benefit from the guidance. These four studies support the use of artificial intelligence to generate explanatory feedback for secondary learners, but they also establish two conditions: the feedback should take the form of hints before answers, and its presentation must be designed with the same care as the diagnosis behind it.

The reviewed literature demonstrates that breadboards remain valuable tools for hands-on electronics learning and that recent developments have improved their flexibility, accessibility, monitoring capabilities, and diagnostic functions. Sun et al. (2022) and Ko et al. (2023) addressed the physical adaptability of breadboards, while Owen et al. (2026) established their instructional value and Boyd and Tow (2026) emphasized accessibility and autonomous circuit construction. Kinsel et al. (2024) and Loubser et al. (2024) established that troubleshooting is a separate competency that learners do not acquire incidentally and that classroom feedback presently under-serves. Bianchi et al. (2024) demonstrated the potential of augmented breadboards to guide and monitor learners, while Calixto et al. (2026) showed how breadboard-based technology can assist with component identification and testing. Faina (2022), Li and Liang (2024), Asbendri et al. (2024), and Moelans et al. (2024) showed that computer-based environments can deliver immediate feedback, improve measurable outcomes, and reduce dependence on the teacher. Zhao et al. (2025), Guo et al. (2025), Dai et al. (2025), and Kimmel et al. (2024) showed that artificial intelligence can generate feedback that improves secondary learners' problem-solving, provided the feedback is hint-based and carefully presented.

Most importantly, the literature provides a basis for automated and immediate feedback. Bianchi et al. (2024) showed that technology-assisted guidance can reduce students' need for teaching-assistant intervention, while Calixto et al. (2026) demonstrated that real-time diagnostic information can help beginners verify components and reduce errors. Taken together, these studies indicate that students benefit from receiving immediate information while performing practical electronics activities rather than depending entirely on teachers for routine problems. The gap, however, lies at the intersection of these developments. Existing systems guide assembly, test isolated components, improve physical access, or simulate circuit behavior, but no reviewed study reports a low-cost trainer, whether physical or computer-based, that reads the connections of a learner's completed circuit, compares them against a teacher-defined correct reference, locates any discrepancy at a specific point on the board, and then uses artificial intelligence to explain the probable cause and guide the learner toward the correction in language a senior high school learner can act upon. The AI feedback studies, for their part, were conducted in programming, physics, and general science rather than in electronics troubleshooting, and none coupled the AI to a deterministic diagnostic system that establishes what the fault actually is before the AI explains it. Two further gaps are contextual, since the reviewed studies were conducted with university, Grade 11, or middle school populations rather than Grade 12 learners in the Technical-Vocational-Livelihood track, and none was situated in the Philippine K to 12 curriculum, where the cost of components commonly obliges learners to work in groups and therefore limits the individual hands-on practice each learner actually receives.


Methodology
(not more than 800 words)

This study will employ a quantitative research design, specifically a descriptive-developmental approach, to develop and evaluate the AI-Integrated Web-Based Smart Diagnostic Breadboard Trainer as an instructional tool. The developmental component covers the design, construction, and refinement of the trainer following the ADDIE model (Analysis, Design, Development, Implementation, Evaluation). The descriptive component measures the trainer's functionality, acceptability, and perceived effectiveness. The approach is appropriate because the study measures the respondents' evaluation of the device against predetermined criteria rather than testing a causal hypothesis.

The participants of the study will be Grade 12 students of a selected public senior high school in the Philippines enrolled in the Technical-Vocational-Livelihood track, electronics specialization. Thirty to forty student-respondents will be selected through purposive sampling based on their ability to participate in the use and evaluation of the trainer. Five to ten TVL teachers handling electronics or a related specialization will provide the instructional perspective, and three to five expert evaluators composed of electronics practitioners or educational technology specialists will assess the functionality of the device, including the accuracy of its AI-generated feedback. The expert group is necessary because learners cannot reliably judge whether a diagnosis or explanation is technically correct.

In development, the researcher will review the K to 12 competencies for Electronic Products Assembly and Servicing and consult TVL teachers to identify the circuits and connection errors learners most commonly encounter. The trainer will be a web-based application running in an ordinary browser on the school's existing computers, built in two layers. The diagnostic layer compares the learner's assembled connections with a teacher-defined reference circuit and, by fixed rules, identifies and locates any discrepancy; it uses no artificial intelligence, so the same circuit always yields the same diagnosis. The feedback layer passes the confirmed fault to a large language model, which explains the probable cause and offers a hint toward the correction at a Grade 12 reading level. Feedback follows a hint-first sequence in which the answer is revealed only on request. Because the model explains a fault the system has already verified, it cannot report a fault that does not exist. Expert evaluators will review sample feedback for each fault type for technical accuracy before the trial. A trial run with learners outside the final sample will precede the formal evaluation, whose results will inform the plan of improvement.

The main instrument will be a researcher-made questionnaire in four parts: respondent profile; functionality, including AI-generated feedback; acceptability in terms of functionality, usability, accuracy, reliability, and instructional value; and perceived effectiveness in developing circuit assembly, component identification, fault identification, and troubleshooting skills. An open-ended portion will gather the problems and difficulties encountered. A four-point Likert scale will be used for all evaluation items, where 4 is Strongly Agree, 3 is Agree, 2 is Disagree, and 1 is Strongly Disagree. The questionnaire will be content-validated by three experts, namely a TLE teacher-educator, an electronics practitioner, and a research or measurement specialist. It will then be pilot-tested on fifteen Grade 12 learners outside the final sample, and its internal consistency will be established using Cronbach's alpha, with a coefficient of 0.70 or higher accepted as reliable.

The researcher will secure written permission from the school administration and coordinate with the subject teacher to schedule the evaluation. Parental consent and learner assent will be obtained before any data is gathered. Respondents will be oriented on the study's purpose, the voluntary nature of participation, and the use of the trainer. They will then perform prescribed circuit activities including at least one deliberately faulted circuit, so the diagnostic and feedback features are actually exercised. After using the trainer, the respondents will answer the researcher-made questionnaire. The completed questionnaires will be collected, checked for completeness, and encoded for statistical analysis.

The collected data will be tabulated and analyzed using appropriate statistical measures. Frequency and percentage will summarize the respondents' distribution and the problems and difficulties reported. The weighted mean and standard deviation will be used to determine the level of functionality, acceptability, and perceived effectiveness of the trainer. The Mann-Whitney U or Kruskal-Wallis H test, as appropriate to the number of groups, will test for significant differences in perceived acceptability across respondent groups at the 0.05 level of significance. Weighted means will be interpreted using the following ranges: 3.26 to 4.00, Strongly Agree or Highly Acceptable; 2.51 to 3.25, Agree or Acceptable; 1.76 to 2.50, Disagree or Moderately Acceptable; and 1.00 to 1.75, Strongly Disagree or Not Acceptable.

Participation will be voluntary and may be withdrawn at any time without consequence. Responses will remain anonymous, be reported only in aggregate, and be stored securely. No personal information about any learner will be sent to the artificial intelligence service, and no respondent's rating will affect any grade.
